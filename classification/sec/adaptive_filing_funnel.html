Adaptive AI Classification Funnel

This is the same retrieval-and-round strategy used to classify every source in this pipeline — SEC filings of every type, and earnings-call/investor-event transcripts. The numbers and charts on this specific page are just one completed slice of that: 127 10-Ks and 20-Fs (annual reports only), ~34,000 possible chunks. Most of any filing is legal boilerplate, financial tables, and risk-factor language that will never mention AI — so instead of classifying every chunk, each document is searched for its most plausible AI-relevant passages, batch-classified a few chunks at a time, and re-evaluated round by round. Documents that stop paying off get dropped; documents still finding real AI content keep going. This run finished naturally after 10 rounds — no document had a nonzero hit rate left.

How one round works

Each round, every document is searched with a specialised financial embedding model against 5 fixed candidate queries, surfacing the top responses per query, per document, per round — merged and deduped before anything is sent to the model. This repeats for as many rounds as a document keeps earning: retrieve → classify in bulk → check the hit rate → keep or drop. Round number is tracked per document, not per batch — a batch submission can mix documents at very different depths, so each chunk's round is recomputed from its own document's history, not inherited from whatever else shipped in the same request.

Documents surviving, round by round

A document only continues if it scored ≥ 2 genuine AI hits in the round just finished — a single stray hit (retrieval noise) isn't enough to justify another round. No document has an artificial ceiling on how many rounds it can run; a fixed per-document chunk cap was tried early on and scrapped once it turned out to be cutting off the highest-signal filings prematurely, well before they'd actually run dry.

Two documents, two outcomes

NVIDIA · 10-K · filed 2013-03-12 0 / 11 candidates hit · dropped after round 1

All 11 of its round-1 candidates — the chunks the search itself judged most likely to be AI-relevant — came back ai_associated = 0. Reading them confirms the search wasn't wrong to give up: this is 2013 Nvidia, a GPU and workstation-graphics company, years before "AI" was any part of how it described itself.

NVIDIA · 10-K · filed 2026-02-25 69 / 194 candidates hit across all 10 rounds (35.6%)

The opposite extreme. Every single round from 1 through 10 kept finding genuine AI content — 13 hits in round 1 alone, still 1 hit left in round 10 before the well finally ran dry. Intel's 2025 10-K told the same story almost exactly (44/204 hits, 21.6%), also surviving the full 10 rounds.

Source: chunk_classifications (round column, computed per document at write time) joined to content_index / content / companies, from classification/sec/LLM_Filtering.py's round-based retrieval over 10-K/20-F filings. Reproducible with: 
select cc.round, count(distinct c.content_id) as docs, count(*) as chunks,
       sum((cc.ai_associated > 0)::int) as hits
from chunk_classifications cc
join content_index ci using (chunk_id)
join content c using (content_id)
where c.type = 'sec_filing' and cc.round is not null
group by 1 order by 1;
Complete run: rounds 1-10, 127 → 59 → 45 → 40 → 33 → 28 → 22 → 17 → 10 → 2 → 0 documents surviving. Two historical bugs were found and fixed mid-run: an early per-document chunk cap (50) that was silently cutting off the highest-signal filings well before they'd actually exhausted their content, and a round-numbering scheme that labeled a whole batch submission with one shared round number even when it mixed documents at different depths — both are reflected in this page's final numbers, not the numbers from before either fix.
